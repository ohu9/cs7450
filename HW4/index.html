A Steep Journey

Global Tea Production, Export, and Trade

Every year, humans drink over 3 billion cups of tea per day. But behind that simple cup lies a complex web of mountain microclimates, production processes, and volatile global markets. Let's trace the journey of the leaf to see who grows it, who drinks it, and who really profits from the steep.

Where Tea is Grown

All tea comes from a single species, Camellia sinensis, with two main varieties: Camellia sinensis sinensis is the small-leafed Chinese variety, adapted to cooler highlands and used primarily for green and white teas. Camellia sinensis assamica is the large-leafed Assam variety, native to the hot, wet lowlands of northeast India, and the backbone of most commercial black tea production. Most estates work with hybrids, but the varietal origin still shapes flavor, yield, and altitude tolerance.

Tea is largely contrained by its growing conditions, which are characterized by temperature, rainfall, altitude, and cloud cover. As a result, only a small fraction of the world's landmass is suitable for tea cultivation. Differences in these conditions and harvesting methods result in the vast diversity of teas we see today.

What's Next: How Tea is Processed

After a tea leaf is plucked, the next 24 hours are crucial. The moment it is separated from the bush, enzymes in the leaf cells begin reacting with oxygen — a process called oxidation. What the processor does next determines everything about what the leaf becomes. There are two main paths:

(1) Halt oxidation — steaming or pan-firing the leaf denatures the enzymes and freezes its chemistry in place, yielding green tea. Handled even more minimally, just dried without rolling, it becomes white tea. Together these account for roughly 39% of global production but command the highest prices per kilogram.

(2) Encourage oxidation — the leaf is rolled or industrially shredded, rupturing its cell walls and accelerating enzyme activity until it turns copper-brown. This produces the black tea that dominates global output at 58% of all production. Oolong occupies the deliberate middle ground, partially oxidized and representing just 3% of global volume, almost entirely from Taiwan and China's Fujian province.

Production vs. Exports: Where Your Tea Comes From

Some countries don't grow tea for themselves — they grow it for the world. The chart to the right compares the production and export quantities of the world's major tea producers.

For China and India, tea is a cultural bedrock before it is a commodity. China produces a staggering volume of tea, yet keeps most of it for domestic consumption. Between traditional ceremonies and a booming modern tea culture, China remains its own best customer. India's tea exports follow a similar trend.

On the flip side, Kenya and Sri Lanka are evidently export-reliant. Kenya exports roughly 93% of everything it grows; Sri Lanka, around 95%. These countries grow tea almost entirely for foreign markets. Nations like Vietnam, Indonesia, and Turkey occupy the middle ground, representing emerging markets where production is scaling alongside local demand.

This chart shows that major producers don't have the most influence in the global market. Contrary to expectation, tea in your mug may not come from the largest tea producers.

The Price of Tea

Tea is predominantly sold at physical auctions, unlike coffee which is traded on a central futures exchange in New York. This chart tracks the price of tea at the world’s top three hubs: Colombo (Sri Lanka), Kolkata (India), and Mombasa (Kenya).

The jagged lines show a market in constant flux: prices are acutely sensitive to weather, politics, and global market shifts. For instance, a massive drought in one region can cause buyers to scramble for tea from another region, causing price spikes across all three auctions.

Disclaimer: The prices in this chart represent the value of tea at its most raw, bulk state, not the price you see at the grocery store. When that same kilogram is processed, branded, and packaged into individual tea bags, its value can skyrocket by 10 to 20 times. Unfortunately, a "stable" price on a chart typically hurts farmers and producers, as their living costs rise while the auction price for their tea remains stagnant.

Conclusion

As we’ve seen, the journey of tea is one of unexpected nuance. From the specific conditions that allow tea to grow, to the way it is processed, to where it is exported, and finally to its price, tea is a complex commodity with a rich history.

As growing regions shift and labor costs rise, the question remains: will the market eventually reflect the true value of the leaf, or will the farmers continue to bear the weight of a stagnant price?
