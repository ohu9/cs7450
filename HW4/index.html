From Leaf to Label

The Journey of Tea

Every year, humans drink over 3 billion cups of tea per day. But the journey from a mountain hillside to your mug is shaped by soil, labor, colonial history, and global commodity markets.

Where Tea is Grown

All tea comes from a single species, Camellia sinensis, with two main varieties: Camellia sinensis sinensis is the small-leafed Chinese variety, adapted to cooler highlands and used primarily for green and white teas. Camellia sinensis assamica is the large-leafed Assam variety, native to the hot, wet lowlands of northeast India, and the backbone of most commercial black tea production. Most estates work with hybrids, but the varietal origin still shapes flavor, yield, and altitude tolerance.

Tea is largely contrained by its growing conditions, which are characterized by temperature, rainfall, altitude, and cloud cover. As a result, only a small fraction of the world's landmass is suitable for tea cultivation. Differences in these conditions and harvesting methods result in the vast diversity of teas we see today.

The Processing Fork in the Road

The same leaf, plucked from the same bush, can become a $40 tin of Silver Needle white tea or a commodity black tea sold by the kilogram at auction. The difference is almost entirely in what happens in the next 24 hours.

The Export Paradox

Some countries don't grow tea for themselves — they grow it for the world. That dependency makes their economies acutely vulnerable to price swings at international auction houses.

The Price a Leaf Gets

A kilogram of bulk black tea fetches roughly the same price at auction today as it did decades ago, adjusted for inflation. Meanwhile, retail value has risen. The leaf's value is captured far from where it grows.

Conclusion

Tea is the world's most democratic drink — cheap, universal, ancient. The next time you steep a bag, remember the journey. Each stop added value — just not necessarily for the people who grew it.
